Introduction

The lecture introduced the principles of measurement, the description of distributions, and the accurate presentation of data. In this practical, these principles are applied to the data collected by the class in Weeks 1 and 2.

The analysis is carried out in Excel for the web. No prior experience of spreadsheets is assumed: the workbook is prepared in advance, and entries are made only in the cells shaded yellow, each confirmed by pressing Enter.

Aim

To locate an individual observation within the class distribution, to compute measures of central tendency and dispersion, to examine within-participant difference scores, and to evaluate the accuracy of two graphical presentations of the same data.

The workbook contains six sheets, listed at the bottom of the screen: Start here, Class data, 1, 2, 3 and 4. Read Start here first, then complete sheets 1 to 4 in order.

TASK 1 / Sheet 1: an individual observation within the class distribution

  1. In the yellow cell, enter your Week 1 block 1 mean reaction time (ms), i.e. the average reported after the first round of five trials. If this value is not available, enter 268.
  2. The corresponding bar of the histogram is highlighted in red.
  3. Note the values reported in the grey cells.

Questions. Where does the observation lie relative to the centre of the distribution? Is the distribution approximately symmetric, or does it have a longer tail on one side?

TASK 2 / Sheet 2: central tendency and dispersion

  1. In the first yellow cell, enter =AVERAGE(times) and press Enter. The adjacent Check cell should read correct.
  2. In the second yellow cell, enter =MEDIAN(times).
  3. In the third yellow cell, enter =STDEV.S(times).
  4. Replace any value in column B with 900 and press Enter. Observe the three yellow cells.
  5. Press Ctrl+Z to restore the original value.

Questions. Which of the three statistics changed most when a single extreme value was introduced, and which changed least? Explain the difference with reference to how each statistic is calculated.

TASK 3 / Sheet 3: within-participant difference scores

  1. Column D contains, for each participant, the block 2 mean minus the block 1 mean.
  2. In the yellow cell, enter =AVERAGE(diffs) and press Enter.
  3. Examine the scatterplot. Each point represents one participant; the dashed line represents identical scores in both blocks.

Questions. Did reaction times change between blocks, on average? How many participants were faster in block 2, and how many slower? To what extent do the points cluster around the line, and what does this indicate about the test–retest reliability of the task?

TASK 4 / Sheet 4: accuracy of graphical presentation

Charts A and B display the same two values from the Week 2 data.

Questions. In which chart does one condition appear considerably slower than the other condition? Compare the starting point of each y-axis. Which chart represents the difference more accurately, and why?

Points for discussion

  • The effect of a single extreme observation on the mean and on the median.
  • Whether a difference of 20 ms between a participant’s two blocks reflects unreliability of the measure or genuine variation in performance.
  • The magnitude of the difference between the auditory and visual conditions in Week 2, and which chart conveys it accurately.

Additional exercises

  • On sheet 4, select the chain labels and the two columns of values (hold Ctrl to select the second range), then select Insert → Chart → Column. Add a title. Confirm whether the y-axis begins at zero.
  • On the Class data sheet, examine the Device column. Do smartphones and laptops appear to differ? Would such a difference constitute random or systematic error?
  • Using the mean and standard deviation from sheet 2, calculate mean − SD and mean + SD. Using the histogram on sheet 1, estimate the proportion of the class falling between these two values.
