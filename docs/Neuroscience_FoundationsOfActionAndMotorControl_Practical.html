Introduction

This practical measures how the time of an aiming movement changes with the precision required, for the dominant and the non-dominant hand.

Being the Tapper is optional. Anyone may act only as the Recorder, without giving a reason. Both roles are needed for the task to work.

Aim

To measure the time taken to move the hand between two targets that require low or high precision, with each hand.

Materials

  • A partner
  • A phone with a stopwatch and a calculator
  • Two empty chairs
  • LINK 1 below, to the form in which results are recorded

TASK 1 · Roles and set-up

Work in pairs. Each pair needs two empty chairs.

  • The Tapper performs the task, standing, and counts the cycles aloud.
  • The Recorder starts and stops the phone stopwatch and writes down the times.

Being the Tapper is optional. Nobody should feel obliged to tap: a student who prefers not to simply acts as the Recorder.

Placing the chairs. Put the two chairs side by side, both facing the Tapper, with a gap of one chair-width between them. The Tapper stands in front of the gap, midway between the two chairs.

Targets. Both targets are the front-left corner of each seat:

  • Wide: the palm comes down flat, covering the corner.
  • Narrow: the tip of the index finger lands on the corner.

Rules.

  • Move the hand from one corner to the other and back, as quickly as possible. One cycle = from the left chair to the right chair and back.
  • The Tapper counts each cycle aloud.
  • Every touch must land on the target. If it does not, correct it before moving on.
  • Touch the chairs lightly: they should not move. If a chair moves, put it back and repeat the trial.

Try each target once, briefly, with each hand.

TASK 2 · Four rounds

Each round has two trials. Complete the rounds in this order:

Round Hand Trials
1 dominant wide, then narrow
2 non-dominant wide, then narrow
3 non-dominant narrow, then wide
4 dominant narrow, then wide

For each trial:

  1. The Recorder says “go” and starts the stopwatch.
  2. The Tapper completes 15 cycles, counting each one aloud.
  3. At “fifteen”, the Recorder stops the stopwatch and writes down the time, to one decimal place (e.g., 18.4 s).

The Recorder keeps the times in a table like this (e.g., in the phone’s notes). Each condition is done twice: its first time is in Round 1 or 2, its second time in Round 3 or 4.

Hand and target First time (s) Second time (s)
Dominant, wide
Dominant, narrow
Non-dominant, wide
Non-dominant, narrow

Then exchange roles and complete the four rounds for the other partner.

TASK 3 · Calculations

Using the phone’s calculator, for each of the four rows of the table:

average time (s) = (first time + second time) ÷ 2

time per movement (ms) = average time ÷ 30 × 1000

Each trial contains 30 movements (15 cycles, each with two movements).

Look at the four times per movement.

  • Which target took longer: wide or narrow?
  • On the narrow target, which hand was slower?
  • Was the difference between wide and narrow larger for one hand than for the other?
  • Were the second times shorter than the first times?

TASK 4 · Submission of results

Each Tapper opens the form at LINK 1 and copies their own eight times from the table, row by row (first time, then second time).

TASK 5 · Class results

The class results are shown on the screen: the average time per movement for each hand and target, across everyone.

Compare your own four times per movement with those of the class.

If the task could not be completed The following times (s; first time, second time) may be used: dominant, wide: 13.8, 13.2; dominant, narrow: 19.5, 18.6; non-dominant, wide: 15.1, 14.6; non-dominant, narrow: 23.4, 22.1.

Take-home points

  • With the same distance and the same location, movements to the narrow target took longer than movements to the wide target, with both hands.
  • The non-dominant hand was slower, especially on the narrow target.
  • The two targets were in the same place, so the only difference between them was the precision required.
  • Times can become shorter with practice. Because each condition was done once early and once late, practice affected all conditions similarly.
  • The stopwatch was started and stopped by a person, and the Tapper counted aloud: both add small errors, similar in every trial.
